# Channel style guide

## Format A: story explainers (DEFAULT from 2026-10-21)
Our red-hood guy (or the girl) lives a tiny story; each thing he tries is a step of how the real thing works; the science slips out in one "you see" line; it ends on a punchline. Not a lecture, not an ad. Looser and more casual than school-accurate: keep the core idea right, don't nitpick. (Xavier's direction.)

Pacing measured from 10 reference shorts Xavier gave as scripts (zack d style, studied for pacing only; never copy their voice, characters or lines):
- **Under 600 characters** (Xavier's limit, about 100 words, 30 to 40 s at his reading pace). 76 to 140 words in the references. About **3.0 words per second**. Written as flowing sentences joined with so / but / then (never chopped fragments like "Nothing. He tried harder. Still nothing."); read with natural pauses at sentence ends. Don't cut breaths or squeeze the timing.
- Deadpan, matter-of-fact narrator even when things get wild. Third person ("he") or second person ("you"), past or present tense. No "we", no "let's", no teaching voice.

### Script shape
1. **Cold open mid-action.** Sentence one is already happening: "He stuffed cotton into his ears as the plane's engines roared to life." No hook question, no "did you know", no intro. (A question opener is fine only when the video IS a question or riddle.)
2. **Chain of events, one picture per clause**, joined with Then / But / So / Now / before / as. Every clause is a new visual, so the edit can cut on every clause.
3. **Each attempt fails in a way that teaches**: cotton (blocks a bit) -> big cups (high sounds stop, low rumble gets through) -> the real tech.
4. **One "You see," pivot** (or "This is because") in the middle: one or two plain sentences of why, then straight back to the story.
5. **Punchline ending, no outro.** End on the consequence, often cheeky or dark: "just in time to hear the baby behind him start screaming." No "congratulations", no call to action, no recap.

### Example (ANC, about 30 s)
> He stuffed cotton into his ears as the plane's engines roared to life. But the roar pushed straight through anyway. So he pulled big headphones over his ears, and the high sounds faded, but the deep rumble kept shaking through. You see, low sounds travel as long, slow waves that pass right through the cushions. So a tiny mic listens to the rumble, and a speaker fires back the exact opposite wave, and the two cancel each other out. The engines went almost silent, just in time to hear the baby behind him start screaming.

### Self-check
- [ ] First sentence is mid-action (no hook line)
- [ ] Every clause is a picture we can cut to
- [ ] Exactly one "you see" style explanation, 1 to 2 sentences
- [ ] Ends on a punchline, no outro
- [ ] 25 to 40 s at about 3 words per second (75 to 120 words)

### Visual rules for Format A
- The character is the main character, on screen most of the time, doing things (not a 1-in-4 cameo).
- **The camera never stops, and it changes the picture on every caption chunk (about every 1 s), but mostly by travelling, not cutting.** Measured on 16 reference shorts: hard cuts only every 1.9 s (median; some videos have 2 or 3 cuts in 30 s), yet 88% of shots move (push-in, pull-out, orbit, crane, dive through a hole into a cut-away, fly from one object to the next) and only 10% of frames are still. Picture change about 10 to 15 per frame.
- **One-take travel between beats**: the camera flies from the person to the object to the inside of the object and back (into the pot, through the pipe, out to the bed), instead of hard-cutting.
- **Show the mechanism on the real object**: x-ray / cut-away of the thing, glowing outlines on the part being talked about, arrows and dotted lines that draw on, a part flashing a colour when named.
- **Captions ONE word at a time** (Xavier, 2026-10-22), white with a dark outline and 3D extrusion, popping on each word.
- Every hold still has motion: slow push-in or drift, plus the character never frozen.
- Poses: R6 limbs swing freely and overlap the body, bodies drop onto props, nothing symmetrical (pose gate).
- Faces do half the acting: expression swap on every beat (kit/facekit.js or the mains' faces), talking mouth shapes while he speaks.

## Format B (legacy): "build it from scratch"

## The format
Every video takes one everyday thing and builds it up from a crude, funny version, one problem at a time, until you have the real thing. 61 to 75 seconds (over 1 minute for TikTok rewards), 9:16, one narrator, word-by-word captions.

## Script grammar (non-negotiable)
Studied from 5 top-performing reference shorts (airplane, rocket, brakes, fridge, engine). Measured: 214 to 304 words, 3.6 to 3.8 words/sec, 6 "But"s, 6 "So you"s and 5 to 6 named parts per script.

The chain: **If you [stunt], you've built a [thing]. But [fail]. → So you [fix]. This is [part], but [new fail]. → So you ... → (that's why / that's the ___ you hear) → Add [x, y and something small and funny]. [Closer].**

### 1. The opener: a funny stunt, not a chore
`If you [crude stunt with real, specific everyday props], you've built a [thing]. But [instant, funny failure].`
- The stunt is a *picture*: "tape a leaf blower to the back of a lawn chair", "drop a Mentos into a Diet Coke in a Walmart parking lot", "stop your car by dragging your feet on the road", "put a firecracker under a soup can". Specific props, specific place. It does not need to be something the viewer would actually do; it needs to make them grin.
- The failure lands in the SAME breath with a concrete, personal consequence: "all you do is scoot across the yard", "it tips over and sprays your truck", "your shoes are smoking and you're still going 50", "the can flies off and you never see it again".
- "you've built a [thing]" within the first 4 seconds. No hook before "If you". No "let's", no "we".

### 2. The rung: fix, name it, break it again (one breath)
`So you [physical verb] ... This is the [real part], but [what goes wrong now].`
- Fuse the name and the next problem into one sentence: "This is the fuselage, but a tube can't fly." "This is lift, but a leaf blower can't get you anywhere near fast enough." That fusion is what makes it feel fast. Never end on a bare "This is X." and restart.
- Fixes use hands-on verbs: bolt, swap, hang, cut, drill, strap, stack, seal, squeeze, wrap. "Swap the X for a Y" is the main escalation move.
- **Name a part only when the name is worth learning** (3 to 5 per video): words people half-know or would want to say (magnetron, compressor, crankshaft, ABS, evaporator). Skip names that add nothing ("this is the cage"). When you do name it, use the real word and keep the sentence around it plain. (Xavier's rule.)
- Each new problem is caused by the last fix: "but now it's just warm gas", "but now it never stops and your lettuce freezes", "all those explosions make the metal crazy hot". Use "now" and "all that".
- Explain the mechanism in one vivid clause of verbs, not a lecture: "sucks in air, squeezes it, sprays in fuel, and lights it on fire."
- Not every rung needs a problem. `Then you add...` is fine for 1 or 2 parts that just belong there.

### 3. Viewer anchors (2 to 3 per script, spread out)
Tie a part to something the viewer has seen, heard or felt. These are the comment and share drivers, more than a single end twist:
- "That's why your ears pop." "It's that hum you hear at night." "That's the click you hear." "That's the part you see stretching out right before you land." "That's why the back of your fridge is warm."
- At least one should bust a belief, placed right after the part that explains it (fridge: "It doesn't make cold. It moves heat into your kitchen. So leaving it open to cool off your house actually makes it hotter."). It does not have to be at the end.

### 4. Texture
- 1 to 2 analogies: "like a dart with no feathers", "like pinching a spinning plate", "slams into the front like a wall".
- 1 to 2 wow numbers: "15 times a second", "dozens of times every single second", "200 seats". These become yellow callouts.
- Personal stakes: your truck, your milk, your lettuce, your ears. The viewer is the one who built it.
- Short sentences, present tense, second person. Casual and a little cheeky, never cringe.

### 5. The close
`Add [2 practical parts] and [1 small funny/humble detail].` then the channel closer. ("Add a cockpit, 200 seats, and a tiny bag of pretzels." "Add a gas tank, a key, and four wheels.")
- Channel closer: `Congratulations, you just built a [thing].` (Keep our own closer. "Look at you, you just built..." is another channel's signature; don't copy it.)

### Length
235 to 265 words for Chatterbox at natural pace (about 65 to 72s). Never write long and rely on the time-stretch.

### Fact rules
Fact-check every mechanism and number with web search; simplify but never say something false; prefer "about" over false precision. No "simply", "basically", "let's", "we", no em dashes.

### Bad vs good opener (standing fan)
BAD: "It's so hot your sweat is sweating. Let's build a standing fan from nothing. Wave your hand." (hook first, instructions, no But/So you)
OK: "If you wave your hand in front of your face, you have a fan. But a minute later your arm is finished." (correct shape, but flat)
GOOD: "If you flap a pizza box lid at your face, you've built a fan. But thirty seconds later your arm is dead and you're sweatier than before."

### Self-check before showing a script
- [ ] Opens "If you [specific funny stunt], you've built a ___. But [instant consequence]."
- [ ] 3 to 5 worthwhile part names, each fused with the next problem ("This is X, but...")
- [ ] Each problem is caused by the previous fix
- [ ] 2 to 3 viewer anchors (that's why / that's the ___ you hear), at least one myth-bust
- [ ] 1 to 2 analogies, 1 to 2 wow numbers
- [ ] No instructions, no "we/let's"; every part introduced before improved
- [ ] Ends "Add [x, y, small funny z]. Congratulations, you just built a ___."
- [ ] 235 to 265 words; every claim fact-checked

## Visual rules
- Stylised 3D, not photoreal. Rounded bevelled shapes, soft shadows, saturated but not neon.
- Cutaway sets: the camera sees through cut walls and soil cross-sections.
- **The object is the star; our guy is the channel's face and comedy.** The red-hoodie guy with the drawn face (kit/hoodie.js, `CHARACTER = 'hoodie'`) is how people recognise the channel. Use him for the stunt (he is the one sitting on the bucket) and for quick reaction beats on fails (half-second cut to his face: nervous, disgusted, confused, proud). Never as decoration, never more than about 1 in 4 shots, never stealing focus from the mechanism. (Xavier's rule.)
- **He is only on screen while he is doing something.** When his action is done he leaves the shot; never park him standing idle. Whenever he is visible he breathes / shifts weight (`breathe()`), walks with `walk()`, never slides.
- **Nothing teleports.** Anything he carries follows his real hands every frame; to put something down he walks to the spot, crouches, lowers it until it touches, lets go, stands. Particles (smoke, stink, steam) go around him, never through his body.
- **Never frame the set's cut edges behind him** (slab, foundation, cut walls): it reads as floating. Shoot him against lawn, bushes, walls.
- **Normal scene by default.** Show mechanisms right on the object in the set: cut it open, zoom in, make the flow visible (glowing water in pipes, arrows, heat colour).
- **Special scene only when the normal scene can't show it**: things too small or invisible (molecules, waves vs tiny holes, heat moving through food). Rule of thumb: at most 2 or 3 special-scene beats per video. (Xavier's rule.)
- **The special scene is its own place**, the channel's "zoom-in world": same look every time (dark blue depth, soft studio light, floating dust), so viewers learn the cue. It is never a blank backdrop: each one is designed for its mechanism.
- **Enter and exit it on purpose**: the camera dives into the real object (into the soup, through the door holes), quick zoom-blur into the special scene; at the end, pull back out to the same spot in the normal scene. Never just hard-cut to blue and back.
- Every problem beat shows the failure happening, every fix beat shows the fix arriving (spring in, slide in, snap on).
- Special scenes use the engine's zoom-in-world backdrop (kit/look.js microInner/microOuter).
- Callouts: yellow face, same outline + 3D extrusion as captions, top third, one line (auto-shrinks), numbers only (`= 9,600 CARS`, `≈ 1 INCH`, `+1°F`).
- Captions: one word at a time, centre screen, **lowercase Montserrat ExtraBold**, white face, thick dark outline, solid 3D extrusion stepping down-right (not a soft shadow). Chosen by Xavier; settings in kit/look.js.
- Never use another channel's name, logo or branding.

## Sound
Voice (Format A, from 2026-10-21): **Xavier records the narration himself**; his clone (`reference/voice/xavier_ref.wav`, Chatterbox) is the fallback. Keep his natural pauses. Renders carry **no sound effects and no music**: deliver a timestamped SFX cue sheet (`out/<name>_sfx.txt`) and he adds sound in his edit.
(Legacy Format B used Gemini TTS + synthesised SFX via sfx.py / mix.sh: old topics only.)

## Retention (nobody scrolls in the first 5 seconds)
- **Cold open**: frame 1 is already mid-action and is the funniest image in the video (usually our guy doing the stunt). No establishing shot, no fade-in.
- **Show the stunt AND the fail by second 4**, don't just say them.
- **Cut cadence**: a cut or big camera move every 1 to 1.5 s for the first 5 s; after that no shot holds longer than about 3 s. Use `subs` (several shots inside one beat) and `whip` transitions.
- **Every "But..." is an event**: punch-in, shake, flash, or a freeze with a record scratch, plus a reaction cut to our guy.
- **Nothing is ever still**: handheld drift is on by default; stink, steam, dust, flies, clouds keep moving.
- **Captions pop** (overshoot on each word); 3 to 5 key words per video are emphasised (bigger, yellow) via `emph`.
- **Sound on every cut** (in the cue sheet): whoosh on whips, hit on punches, scratch on freezes.
- **Boredom check is a gate**: `python3 tools/boredom.py out/<topic>_draft.mp4` must say HOOK OK and no BORING STRETCH before the final render.

## Lighting (designed, not default)
- `CINEMA = true` in every new topic: key + fill + a **rim light behind the subject** on every shot, warm key / cool shadows, bloom on real highlights only, colour grade, vignette.
- **Lighting moods are story beats** (kit/cinema.js MOODS): `morning` for the stunt, `golden` for reveals and the ending, `gross` (sick green) when something smells or goes wrong, `spot` (dark, single light) for "here's how it actually works" moments, `interior` indoors. Cue them per beat (`mood`) or mid-beat (`moodCues`).

## THE TICKLE RECIPE (Xavier 2026-10-24: "our tickle video did REALLY well, 100% retention, whatever we did there, keep it up")
Every new video copies this until something beats it. Reference: topics/tickle/ (script.json + topic.js), 37.7 s.
1. TOPIC: an everyday "why does my body do that" question viewers have personally felt ("why can't you tickle yourself").
2. BEAT 1, cold open mid-action (0-5 s): the main guy is already doing the thing in a relatable place (bored in a class test, tries to tickle himself, nothing happens). No hook line, no question, no intro. LOCKED 2026-11-14 (Xavier, from his view counts: tickle 18K, hypnic 8.6K): open on him IN CLASS ON HIS CHAIR doing something, and line 1 starts "He ..." in third person ("He got bored in class...", "He was just dozing off in class..."). This replaces the 2026-11-12 spoken-question hook and second-person scripts.
3. BEAT 2, escalation (5-13 s): he tries harder, three quick escalating attempts (armpit, ribs, feet), each its own pose and shot. Still nothing.
4. BEAT 3, "You see" (13-29 s): fly INTO his head to Brain HQ, a little version of him running the controls; the science is acted out as a visual gag (screen predicts his own hand, he pulls the TICKLE lever, relaxes... then UNKNOWN FINGER, alarm, panic). Show it, don't diagram it.
   NEVER a science-show explainer (Xavier 2026-10-24, from the retention graphs: viewers scroll away the moment the video turns into a blue glowing x-ray / diagram / lab look). The explanation is always CHARACTERS ACTING inside a place (Brain HQ crew pulling levers, little him reacting to screens, workers inside the body), with a gag, in the same bright Roblox look as the rest of the video.
5. BEAT 4, payoff (29-38 s): back in the real world the cause hits from outside (the girl pokes him mid-test), the BIG reaction (he screams), and the whole room reacts (class turns round, each classmate individually); she whistles innocently. Ends on the joke, no outro.
6. Craft: Xavier's own voice take, one-word captions, a cut or camera move every ~2 s (19 cuts in 38 s), wider cameras, faces matched to every emotion (bored, frustrated, squint, confused, scream), furniture moves physically, background people react individually, no dead second (boredom.py HOOK OK, no boring stretch).

## DIRECTING LIKE A MOVIE (Xavier 2026-10-24, growl video)
- Treat every short as a comedy movie: the camera changes every 1-2 s (cut, punch-in, whip, drone pull-back); no shot sits while the words carry it.
- Act the setup out literally (skipped breakfast = him sprinting out the door, then the untouched omelette + bacon on the table).
- Let jokes land: after a big beat (the first growl) leave a beat of SILENCE on screen with a big reaction shot (drone pull-back over the whole frozen class).
- Cut away to reaction faces constantly: his pain / shock faces, the girl next to him staring like he is in labour, the class turning, the teacher.
- Use POV shots for embarrassment (his POV as the class turns to stare).
- Anticipation: before a punchline, show him sensing it coming (eyes snap open, freeze).
- The explainer workers / micro world get 2-3 s max per beat; cut back to the real character reacting in between.
- Ending: go BIG and wide (aerial outside the building as the final growl hits).
