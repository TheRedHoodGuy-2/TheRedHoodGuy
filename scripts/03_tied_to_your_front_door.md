# Everyone Is Tied To Their Front Door 🚪

STATUS: draft for Xavier. Not approved.
Cast: narrator (red hood kid), best friend Milo, Mom, neighbour, mailman, grown-ups, teachers, grandmas.
Places: hilltop house and porch, street, playground, pond with a dock, inside the house.
Rule props: a rope from every belt to a doorknob, the front door (comes off its hinges), a spoon, a kite.
Plant: he fixes things at home and can take the squeaky door off its hinges with a spoon. Loophole: he is tied to the DOOR, and the door does not have to stay on the house.

## Script
In this town, everyone is tied to their own front door. You see, on the day you're born, a rope grows from your belt to your doorknob, and it never comes off. Kids get twenty steps of rope. Grown-ups get a hundred. And if you go past the end, it snaps you home like a yo-yo. So our whole town is one big knot. The mailman has to jump rope down every street. Dog walkers get tied up twice a day. And my mom and our neighbor have been stuck in the same tangle since Tuesday.

But my rope is the shortest in town. Our house is at the top of the hill, so I can't even reach the playground. Every day, I stand at the end of my rope and watch the other kids on the swings. So I stay home and fix things. Wobbly chairs. Leaky sinks. And our squeaky front door. I've taken it off its hinges so many times, I can do it with a spoon.

Until one morning, my best friend Milo was flying his kite by the pond. The wind grabbed it, and pulled him right off the dock. His rope went tight. He was hanging at the end of it, in the deep water.

The grown-ups ran. But every one of them snapped back like a yo-yo, ten steps short. Somebody get a longer rope! There are no longer ropes. Milo's head went under.

I ran to the end of mine. Twenty steps. Not even close.

And that's when I looked back at my house. The rule says I'm tied to my front door. It never said the door has to stay on the house.

So I ran home, grabbed a spoon, and popped the hinges. One. Two. Three. And I ran down that hill with my front door on my back. Past the playground. Past the swings. Past every grown-up in town. I threw the door into the pond like a raft, jumped on, and paddled with the spoon. I grabbed Milo by the hood and pulled him up. He coughed out a fish. A small one.

Everybody cheered. Then they all turned and looked at their own doors.

The next morning, the mailman walked his whole route with a door under his arm. Then the teachers. Then the grandmas. Now nobody in this town is stuck at home. Our houses are just very, very cold.
